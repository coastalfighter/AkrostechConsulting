Most hiring delays happen between steps, not during them: an interview that takes four days to schedule, feedback that sits in an inbox, a candidate who hears nothing for a week. A dedicated recruiting coordinator removes that waiting time by owning the logistics, and an offshore coordinator who works your U.S. hours does it at a lower cost than adding local headcount. The result is a shorter time-to-hire without asking your recruiters or hiring managers to work faster.

Here is where the time goes and how a coordinator gets it back.

## Where does time-to-hire actually go?

Map a single candidate's journey and count the days where something is happening versus the days where they are waiting. For many teams, the waiting days outnumber the working days. The usual culprits:

- **Scheduling ping-pong.** Several emails to find a time that works for a candidate and a panel.
- **Feedback delays.** Interviewers forget to submit scorecards, so the next step cannot be booked.
- **Status silence.** Candidates go days without an update and accept other offers.
- **ATS lag.** Records are out of date, so nobody knows who is waiting on what.
- **Handoff gaps.** The recruiter is in back-to-back screens and cannot move candidates forward.

None of these need a senior recruiter. They need someone whose whole job is to keep the pipeline moving.

## What does a recruiting coordinator own?

A coordinator takes the logistics off your recruiters' plates so they can spend more time screening and advising hiring managers.

| Coordinator task | Delay it removes |
| --- | --- |
| Interview scheduling across calendars | Days of back-and-forth email |
| Confirmations and reminders | No-shows and last-minute reschedules |
| Feedback chasing within 24 hours | Stalled decisions after interviews |
| Candidate status updates | Drop-off from candidates who feel ignored |
| ATS stage updates and notes | Confusion about who is next |
| Interview kit preparation | Interviewers walking in unprepared |
| Offer paperwork and background check requests | Slow close after the decision |
| Weekly pipeline report | Bottlenecks spotted too late |

## Why does time-zone alignment matter so much?

Coordination is real-time work. If a candidate replies with availability at 11 a.m. Eastern and your coordinator is offline until the next morning, you have lost a day. Multiply that across every step and every candidate and the delays compound.

Akrostech recruiting staff work your chosen U.S. time zone, EST, CST, MST or PST, so they are online when candidates and hiring managers are. That is the difference between an offshore coordinator who speeds things up and one who adds lag.

## How do you set up a coordinator for success?

### 1. Write scheduling rules once

Document the rules your coordinator should follow without asking:

- Interview stages and who sits on each panel
- Preferred interview times and blackout periods for each interviewer
- Buffer time between interviews
- Video platform and standard invitation text
- What to do when an interviewer cancels

### 2. Set feedback service levels

Agree that interviewers submit scorecards within one business day, and give the coordinator permission to remind them. Feedback delays are often the single largest source of waiting in the funnel.

### 3. Standardize candidate communication

Create templates for every stage: application received, screen booked, interview confirmed, next steps, decision. Personalize the name and role; keep the structure consistent.

### 4. Give the right access

Named ATS and calendar accounts with permissions to schedule, update stages and add notes. Keep offer approval and compensation data restricted to the people who need it.

### 5. Report weekly

A short report covering candidates by stage, average days in each stage and the oldest open items. This is where bottlenecks become visible.

## Which metrics should you watch?

Pick a few and track them weekly from the first week, so you have a baseline to compare against.

1. **Days from application to first screen.**
2. **Days from screen to first interview.**
3. **Days from final interview to decision.**
4. **Interview no-show and reschedule rate.**
5. **Offer acceptance rate.**
6. **Overall time-to-hire,** measured from pipeline entry to accepted offer.

Do not chase a single headline number. Look at days per stage. If one stage is consistently slow, that is where to focus.

### An illustrative example

Illustrative example: a healthcare staffing team finds that candidates wait several days between a successful phone screen and their first interview because the recruiter books every interview personally. They add an offshore coordinator on CST hours who books interviews the same day the screen is passed, sends reminders the day before and chases feedback the morning after. The recruiter spends that recovered time on more screens, and the screen-to-interview stage shrinks noticeably within the first month.

## What should stay with your in-house team?

A coordinator moves the process along; they do not make hiring decisions. Keep these with your recruiters and hiring managers:

- Deciding who advances after an interview
- Compensation and offer approval
- Sensitive candidate conversations, such as a counteroffer or a rejection after a final round
- Hiring manager calibration on what good looks like

## Key takeaways

- Most time-to-hire is waiting between steps, not work within them.
- A recruiting coordinator owns scheduling, reminders, feedback chasing, status updates and ATS hygiene.
- Offshore coordinators only speed things up if they work your U.S. hours.
- Written scheduling rules, feedback service levels and templates make the role effective from week one.
- Measure days per stage, not just overall time-to-hire, to find the real bottleneck.
