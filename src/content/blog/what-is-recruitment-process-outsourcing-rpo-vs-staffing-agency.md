Recruitment process outsourcing (RPO) is a long-term arrangement where an outside team becomes part of your talent acquisition function, owning some or all of the recruiting work: sourcing, screening, scheduling, candidate communication and offer coordination. Unlike a staffing agency, which is typically paid per placement, an RPO partner works inside your process and is measured on the whole pipeline. It fits best when you have ongoing or high-volume hiring and need capacity faster than you can build it.

Here is how it works, how it compares with the alternatives and how to tell whether you are ready.

## How does RPO work in practice?

An RPO engagement starts with an intake. You and the partner agree on role profiles, scorecards, service-level targets and who owns each step. From there, the RPO team works in your applicant tracking system (ATS), under your employer brand, alongside your hiring managers.

A typical division of work looks like this:

1. **Intake and role calibration.** Job descriptions, must-have skills and interview stages are agreed.
2. **Sourcing.** Recruiters search job boards, professional networks and talent databases, and run outreach.
3. **Screening.** Structured phone screens against the scorecard, with notes logged in your ATS.
4. **Scheduling and coordination.** Interviews booked, reminders sent, feedback chased.
5. **Offer support.** Offer letters prepared, references checked, start dates coordinated.
6. **Reporting.** Funnel conversion, time-to-fill and source quality shared on a regular cadence.

## RPO vs. staffing agency vs. in-house team

Each model solves a different problem. This comparison shows where they differ.

| Factor | Staffing agency | RPO partner | In-house recruiting team |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Engagement | Role by role | Ongoing program | Permanent headcount |
| Typical pricing basis | Fee per placement | Monthly fee, per-hire fee or a blend | Salaries, tools and benefits |
| Employer brand | Agency's name often leads | Your brand throughout | Your brand |
| Process ownership | Agency's process | Your process, run by the partner | Your process |
| Data and candidate pipeline | Often stays with the agency | Lives in your ATS | Lives in your ATS |
| Scales up and down | Per role | Recruiter capacity flexes with plans | Slow to change headcount |
| Best for | Occasional, hard-to-fill roles | Repeat or high-volume hiring | Stable, predictable hiring |

Pricing varies widely by provider and scope, so compare proposals on what is included, not just the headline number.

### Why the pipeline ownership point matters

With an agency, the candidates who were not hired often stay in the agency's database. With RPO, every sourced and screened candidate is logged in your ATS. Over time, that becomes a talent pool you own and can reuse for future roles.

## What are the signs you are ready for RPO?

You do not need to be a large company. You need a hiring problem that repeats. Common signals:

- **Several open roles at once,** or a hiring plan for the next two or three quarters.
- **Hiring managers doing their own sourcing** and falling behind on their day jobs.
- **Slow scheduling.** Candidates wait days for an interview time.
- **Agency fees adding up** across roles you hire for again and again.
- **Inconsistent screening.** Different interviewers use different criteria for the same role.
- **A small recruiting team** that cannot absorb a spike in demand.

If you hire one or two people a year, an agency or a direct search is probably simpler. If hiring is constant, RPO usually gives you more control.

## What can you outsource, and what should you keep?

RPO does not have to be all or nothing. A common approach is to start with the steps that consume the most hours and keep the steps that need your judgment.

**Commonly outsourced**

- Sourcing and outreach
- Resume screening and phone screens
- Interview scheduling and reminders
- Candidate status updates
- Reference checks and offer paperwork
- ATS data hygiene and reporting

**Commonly kept in-house**

- Final interviews and the hiring decision
- Compensation strategy and offer approval
- Culture and team-fit conversations
- Employer brand positioning

## How do you evaluate an RPO partner?

Ask these questions in every proposal conversation:

1. **Who will actually do the work?** Named recruiters, or a rotating pool?
2. **Which industries do they recruit for?** Domain knowledge shortens calibration.
3. **Will they work in your ATS and under your brand?**
4. **What are the SLAs?** For example, time from intake to first shortlist.
5. **What reporting will you see, and how often?**
6. **Can you scale capacity up and down** as your hiring plan changes?
7. **Will they work your hours?** Real-time collaboration with hiring managers matters.

### An illustrative example

Illustrative example: a software company plans to hire a dozen engineers and support staff over two quarters. Its single in-house recruiter is overloaded with scheduling. The company engages an RPO partner for sourcing, screening and scheduling, while the in-house recruiter focuses on hiring-manager alignment, final interviews and offers. Every candidate is logged in the company's ATS, building a pipeline for the next round of hiring.

## Key takeaways

- RPO is an ongoing partnership that embeds recruiters in your process, not a per-placement service.
- Agencies suit occasional, hard-to-fill roles; RPO suits repeat or high-volume hiring.
- With RPO, candidate data stays in your ATS and your brand leads every conversation.
- Start with sourcing, screening and scheduling; keep final decisions and offers in-house.
- Evaluate partners on named recruiters, SLAs, reporting and time-zone alignment.
