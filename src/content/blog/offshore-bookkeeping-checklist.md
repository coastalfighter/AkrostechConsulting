Outsourcing bookkeeping offshore works when the setup is right and fails when it is improvised. The difference is almost never the bookkeeper's skill. It is whether access, scope and review were agreed before the first transaction was coded.

Use this checklist to move your books to an [offshore accounting team](/services/accounting-assistance) without losing control of your data or your close.

## 1. Define the scope in writing

List exactly what the team will own. A typical starting scope:

- Daily transaction coding and bank feed review
- Bank and credit card reconciliations
- Bill entry and scheduled payment runs, with your approval
- Customer invoicing and cash application
- Month-end accruals and prepaid schedules
- A monthly close pack for your review

Be equally clear about what stays with you: approving payments, signing tax returns, and decisions on accounting policy. Filing and attestation stay with your licensed CPA.

## 2. Review the books before handing them over

Ask for a short books review first. In two days a good team can tell you which accounts do not reconcile, how far behind the books are, and what needs a one-off clean-up before monthly work starts. Fix the past before you build the routine.

## 3. Set up access properly

This is where most of the risk sits, and most of it is avoidable.

- **Named user accounts.** Every bookkeeper gets their own login in QuickBooks, Xero or NetSuite. Never share a password.
- **Least privilege.** Give each person the role they need and nothing more. A bookkeeper rarely needs to add bank accounts or users.
- **Multi-factor authentication** on every account that touches your money.
- **No direct bank login.** Bank feeds and read-only statements cover almost every bookkeeping task. Payments go through an approval tool such as Bill.com, where you release the money.
- **A password manager** for any credential that must be shared, so it can be revoked without changing it everywhere.
- **An offboarding step.** Access is removed the same day an engagement or a person changes.

## 4. Put confidentiality in writing

Every person touching your books should be covered by a non-disclosure agreement before day one. Ask your provider how they vet staff, where work is done, and whether files can be downloaded to personal devices. The answer should be no.

## 5. Agree a close calendar

A close calendar turns month-end from a scramble into a routine. For each business day after month-end, list the tasks, the owner and the reviewer.

| Business day | Task | Owner |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Bank and card reconciliations | Bookkeeper |
| 2 | Accounts payable and receivable cut-off | Bookkeeper |
| 3 | Accruals, prepaids and payroll entries | Accountant |
| 4 | Balance sheet reconciliations and variance notes | Accountant |
| 5 | Close pack to controller or owner | Senior reviewer |

Start with the calendar you can hit, then tighten it.

## 6. Build in review

Offshore or not, books need a second pair of eyes. Ask for:

- A senior review of every close before it reaches you.
- A list of open questions each month, rather than guesses.
- Reconciliation reports for every balance sheet account.

## 7. Choose the right working hours

A bookkeeper who works your U.S. hours can ask a question about a transaction and get an answer the same day. That alone can take days off the close. At Akrostech every accountant works a fixed U.S. time-zone shift for this reason.

## 8. Measure it

Pick three numbers and review them monthly:

- Business days to close
- Unreconciled balances at month-end
- Review notes per close

If those improve over the first three months, the setup is working.

## The short version

Scope it, review the books, lock down access, put confidentiality in writing, agree a calendar, build in review, overlap your hours and measure. Do those eight things and offshore bookkeeping is as safe as an in-house hire, and usually more consistent.
